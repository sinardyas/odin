Lesson 6 · Your way in

AWS Client VPN: reaching a server with no public IP

The whole design keeps the internet out. This is how you still get in — the VPN you asked about on day one.

Where this sits → This is the second journey on the blueprint (Ⓐ→Ⓑ) and the reason you started: the app servers have no public IP and their security group only trusts the ALB (Lesson 5). So how do you SSH in to debug or patch? Not by weakening any of that — by tunnelling inside the network with Client VPN.

1. The bind we've deliberately created

Every layer so far worked to make the app servers unreachable from the internet: private subnet (Lesson 1), no 0.0.0.0/0 → igw route (Lesson 2), a security group that only accepts the ALB (Lesson 5). That's exactly what we wanted for users — and it locks you out too. You need admin access without punching a hole the internet could crawl through.

The tempting-but-wrong fixes:

  • Give the server a public IP → throws away the entire private design.
  • A bastion / jump host (a small public server you SSH into first) → works, but it's a machine you now have to patch and defend, and it's a permanent target on the internet.

2. Client VPN puts your laptop inside the VPC

An AWS Client VPN is a managed endpoint your laptop connects to with a normal OpenVPN client. Once connected, your laptop is handed a private address within the VPC's world — as if it were plugged into the network directly. From there you reach 10.0.11.5 over the same local route any internal resource uses (Lesson 2). Nothing was exposed to the internet; you joined the private side.[aws]

The mental model A bastion host means "SSH to a public machine, then hop inward." Client VPN means "become part of the private network, then talk to anything directly." No public jump box to run, patch, or defend.

3. The four things that define a Client VPN

The endpoint
The managed VPN server AWS runs for you. Your laptop's OpenVPN client dials this.
Target network (association)
A subnet in your VPC you associate the endpoint with — this wires it into your routing so connected clients can reach the VPC.[aws]
Client CIDR range
A separate IP range (e.g. 172.16.0.0/22) handed out to connecting laptops. It must not overlap your VPC's range — remember CIDR from Lesson 1.
Authentication + authorization rules
Who may connect (a client certificate, or a company login via SAML/Active Directory) and, once in, which parts of the VPC they're allowed to reach (e.g. "allow 10.0.11.0/24").[aws]

4. It still has to pass the firewall (Lesson 5 returns)

Being inside the network isn't a free pass. Your traffic to the app server still hits that server's security group. So the last wiring step is a security-group rule on the app server: allow SSH (port 22) from the Client VPN — sourced from the client CIDR range or the VPN's own security group. Two independent gates — the VPN authorizes you onto the network, the security group permits the specific port — and that layering is the point.

Not to be confused with… Client VPN connects individual people/laptops into the VPC — that's you. Site-to-Site VPN connects an entire network (an office, another datacenter) to the VPC. You wanted the first one; this lesson is it.
Cost radar — two meters Client VPN bills per subnet-association hour (the endpoint being wired into a subnet) and per active connection hour (each connected laptop).[aws] The association meter runs even when nobody's connected — so people often associate it only when admin access is needed, or accept the standing cost for always-on access.

Check yourself

From memory — interleaving Lessons 1, 2, and 5.

Q1. What does connecting through a Client VPN actually do for your laptop?
Correct. You're handed a private address in the VPC's world and reach servers over the local route — nothing is exposed to the internet.
It puts your laptop inside the private network, reaching servers over the local route. No public IP is added and no inbound internet route is opened — that's the whole appeal.
Q2. After connecting, you still can't SSH to the app server. Most likely missing piece?
Correct. Being on the network isn't permission — the server's security group (Lesson 5) still gates the port. Add an allow-22-from-VPN rule.
The security group still gates the port. Being inside the network doesn't bypass it — add an "allow 22 from the VPN" rule. No IGW or subnet move is needed.
Q3. Why must the client CIDR range not overlap the VPC's range?
Correct. Clients get addresses from a separate range; if it overlapped the VPC, an address could mean two places at once and routing couldn't decide where to send traffic.
It's about routing: overlapping ranges would make an address ambiguous, so traffic couldn't be routed reliably. It's not a pricing or subnet-size rule.
